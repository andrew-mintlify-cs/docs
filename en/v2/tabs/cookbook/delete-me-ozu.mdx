Ozu Castle (大洲城, _Ōzu-jō_) is a Japanese hilltop castle overlooking the Hijikawa River in Ozu, Ehime Prefecture, on the island of Shikoku. Its four-story wooden main keep was faithfully reconstructed in 2004 using traditional joinery, making it one of the few post-war castle keeps in Japan rebuilt entirely from wood rather than concrete.

## Quick facts

- **Location:** Ozu, Ehime Prefecture, Shikoku, Japan
- **First built:** circa 1331 (Kamakura period)
- **Reconstructed:** 2004 (main keep)
- **Style:** Japanese hilltop castle (_hirayama-jiro_)
- **Original clans:** Utsunomiya, later Todo Takatora and the Kato clan
- **Current use:** Museum and cultural landmark; overnight "castle stay" experience

## History

The site has been fortified since the 14th century, when the Utsunomiya clan established a stronghold on Jizogatake hill. The castle was expanded in the late 16th century by Todo Takatora, the renowned castle architect, and later became the seat of the Kato clan for most of the Edo period. The original keep was dismantled in 1888 during the Meiji era's wave of castle demolitions.

## Reconstruction

The 2004 reconstruction is notable for its authenticity. Rather than pouring concrete, the project used traditional Japanese carpentry techniques, hand-planed hinoki cypress, and joinery methods documented in Edo-period drawings and photographs. It is the tallest wooden castle keep rebuilt in Japan in the modern era.

## Castle stay

Since 2020, Ozu Castle has offered one of Japan's only "castle stay" (_shirotomari_) programs, in which a small number of guests per night sleep inside the main keep and experience a torch-lit lord's welcome ceremony. The program is part of a wider effort to revitalize the historic town of Ozu.
