## Overview

The capybara is a semiaquatic herbivore native to every South American country except Chile. It inhabits savannas, wetlands, and dense forests, always living near freshwater such as rivers, lakes, ponds, and swamps. The name comes from the Tupi *ka'apiûara*, meaning "one who eats slender leaves," or more simply, "grass-eater."

Together with the lesser capybara, it makes up the genus *Hydrochoerus*. Its closest relatives are guinea pigs and rock cavies, with more distant kinship to agoutis, chinchillas, and nutrias.

## Description

- **Length:** 106 to 134 cm (3.5 to 4.4 ft)
- **Height at withers:** 50 to 62 cm (20 to 24 in)
- **Weight:** typically 35 to 66 kg (77 to 146 lb); females are slightly heavier than males
- **Build:** heavy, barrel-shaped body with a broad head and blunt muzzle
- **Fur:** reddish-brown on top, yellowish-brown underneath, coarse and lacking underhair
- **Feet:** slightly webbed, with four toes on the front and three on the rear

Their eyes, ears, and nostrils sit near the top of the head, an adaptation for spending long periods in water.

## Ecology and behavior

Capybaras are superb swimmers and can hold their breath underwater for up to five minutes. A typical day involves resting on dry land in the morning, cooling off in the water during the hot afternoon, and grazing through the late afternoon and night.

As herbivores, they graze mainly on grasses and aquatic plants, supplemented with fruit and tree bark. They are highly selective feeders and shift their diet between wet and dry seasons. Like guinea pigs, they cannot synthesize vitamin C. They practice autocoprophagy (eating their own feces) to extract more nutrition from cellulose-heavy plants.

Predators include jaguars, ocelots, cougars, eagles, caimans, and green anacondas. Wild capybaras typically live about four years despite a maximum lifespan of 8 to 10 years, or up to 12 years in captivity.

## Social life

Capybaras are gregarious, usually living in groups of 10 to 20 with two to four adult males, four to seven adult females, and juveniles. During the dry season, groups can gather into aggregations of 50 to 100 individuals around shared water sources.

They communicate with barks, chirps, whistles, huffs, and purrs. Males scent-mark using a gland on the snout called a morrillo, as well as anal glands. Mating takes place only in water, and females have the mating choice, so an uncooperative female simply submerges or leaves the water.

Gestation lasts 130 to 150 days and produces a litter of about four pups. Alloparenting is common: young may suckle from any female in the group until weaning at around 16 weeks.

## Conservation and human interaction

The IUCN lists the capybara as Least Concern; populations are stable across most of its range. It is hunted for meat and hide and, in some areas, farmed. In parts of Venezuela, capybara meat is traditionally eaten during Lent and Holy Week under a longstanding Catholic dispensation.

Capybaras have adapted well to urban environments and are common in zoos and parks. They are famously docile, and their calm demeanor has earned them large fandoms in Japan and China, where footage of capybaras bathing in hot springs at zoos such as Izu Shaboten Zoo has drawn millions of views.
